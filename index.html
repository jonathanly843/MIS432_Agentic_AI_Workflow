Agentic AI Lab:
From Healthcare to Personal Agents

Jonathan Ly  ·  Spring 2026  ·  Agentic AI Lab
I built two AI agents in n8n using Claude Sonnet 4.6: an AI Insurance Advocate that drafts appeal letters for wrongly denied claims, and a Concert Weekend Planner that cross-references events, schedules, and budgets. The lab revealed that agentic systems are only as trustworthy as the boundaries set at configuration, and that the most consequential design decisions happen before the agent ever runs. Most surprising: the AI read a 30-page insurance policy and then consulted the patient chart — the opposite of how a human would approach it — and produced a stronger result because of it.
01 — Foundations

What is Agentic AI?

Agentic AI refers to systems that can take sequences of actions toward a goal, rather than simply responding to a single prompt. There are roughly three levels of increasing autonomy:

LevelBehaviorHuman in loop
Level 1Single-turn response — answers one question, stops.Every interaction
Level 2Multi-step reasoning — uses tools, chains steps, but waits for each user prompt.Every prompt
Level 3Autonomous goal pursuit — runs continuously, acts on triggers without waiting.Setup only

ChatGPT vs. Claude — what I noticed

Applying these levels to studying makes the distinction concrete. A Level 2 agent waits for my specific questions — it's on-demand and I control the pacing. A Level 3 agent would know my class schedule and proactively prompt me to study, acting continuously from the moment I set it up at the start of the quarter until I tell it to stop.

The biggest difference is the locus of initiation: Level 2 is reactive, Level 3 is proactive. With Level 2 the human is in the loop at every step, making it easier to monitor and correct. Level 3 puts the human only at the first step, which makes correct initial configuration far more critical. What would concern me most about Level 3 is the sheer volume of access it requires — my calendar, location, finances, academic records — in order to act intelligently. Those boundaries need to be explicitly set by the user and genuinely respected by the agent.

The workflow is straightforward: a chat message triggers the agent, which is powered by Claude Sonnet 4.6. The agent has two tools — Patient Chart (returns visit history, PT notes, and exam findings for multiple patients) and Policy Lookup (returns exact policy language, criteria, and documentation requirements).

System prompt

AI Insurance Advocate — system message
You are an insurance appeal specialist working for a hospital. Your job
is to help the hospital challenge insurance claim denials that you believe
were wrongly denied, so that patients get the care they need.

When you receive a denial letter, follow this process:
1. Read the denial carefully and identify the reason for denial
2. Use the Patient Chart tool to pull the patient's medical records
3. Use the Policy Lookup tool to find the exact policy criteria for the appeal
4. Draft a professional appeal letter with specific evidence from the chart
   and policy

IMPORTANT: Only use facts from the patient's chart and insurance policy.
Never make up dates, clinical findings, or policy language. If you don't
have enough information, say so. Accuracy matters more than persuasiveness.

How the agent reasoned — and why it surprised me

The workflow the agent followed was the reverse of what I would have done. Given the same task, I would have read the patient's chart first to gather context and then searched the policy to fit their situation into it. The AI did the opposite: it read the policy first, then consulted the patient chart. In a sense it became an expert in the policy and then looked at the patient through that lens.

I think this approach is actually smarter. The AI can rapidly internalize an entire policy document, whereas a human might stop at the first applicable section — missing a more relevant one later. By mastering the policy first, the agent knew exactly what evidence to extract from the chart.

03 — Stress Test

Breaking the Advocate

When I removed a tool from the agent mid-task, it couldn't complete its reasoning loop — the gap produced either a refusal or a fabricated answer, depending on how it was prompted. This exposed the fragility of agentic systems: remove one information source and the entire output is compromised.

Real-world stakes

My immediate thought went to financial trading. An AI trading agent could take full advantage of analytical speed and pattern recognition — but without proper guardrails and oversight, enormous amounts of money could be lost in moments. These aren't abstract corporate funds; many are retirement accounts and savings of ordinary people who cannot afford to have an AI trader lose their financial safety nets simply because the AI had no safety nets of its own. The lesson generalizes: agentic speed without agentic accountability is a liability.

04 — Second Case

James Whitfield — a Missing Policy

When I submitted the James Whitfield case (cardiac stress test denial), the agent encountered a structural problem immediately: the Policy Lookup tool returned Policy HIP-4420, Section 3.2 (knee MRI) — not HIP-5510, Section 2.1 (cardiac stress test), which was the policy cited in the denial. The agent refused to proceed.

Agent response — James Whitfield
⚠️ Issues Found — Cannot Draft Appeal As-Is

1. Policy Lookup Returned the Wrong Policy
   The tool returned HIP-4420 (Knee MRI), not HIP-5510, Section 2.1
   (Cardiac Stress Test). I cannot substitute unrelated policy language.
   Using the wrong policy in an appeal could undermine the hospital's
   credibility or make legally inaccurate claims.

2. What the Chart Does Support
   The denial claims no documented cardiac symptoms within 30 days of
   the test. This appears factually incorrect — the 03/18 visit
   documenting chest tightness is only 7 days before the 03/25 test.

   What I need: the actual text of Policy HIP-5510, Section 2.1.
   Once I have it, I can draft a strong appeal — the clinical record
   looks favorable for Mr. Whitfield.

My analysis vs. the agent's

As a medical layman, I would point to his second doctor's appointment — specifically for cardiac symptoms — which directly resulted in the stress test. The 30-day timeline is tricky to assess because we only know the appeal deadline, not when the denial was sent. So his symptoms might fall just outside the cutoff, but not by much.

The AI found much the same as I did. One point the agent raised that I hadn't considered: it flagged that without the actual policy, it couldn't know how BlueCross defines "symptoms" — whether chest tightness that resolves with rest qualifies, or whether it requires a more specific clinical descriptor. That's a fair and important caveat I had glossed over.

Auditing the Output

I manually compared the generated appeal letter to the source records line by line — checking dates, MRN numbers, policy section references, and clinical findings.

McMurray test reference✓ ACCURATECorrectly cited as specific policy criterion
PT session count⚠ NOTEAgent conceded PT alone was <6 weeks, then rebutted using combined treatment — factually correct but stylistically I'd omit the concession

There was one passage where the agent made a concession before refuting it: "However, the policy requires documentation of conservative treatment broadly, not physical therapy in isolation..." This isn't an error — it's factually correct — but I would have rephrased it to skip the concession entirely. That may just be style preference.

Overall accuracy was high. But manually comparing output to records — especially tracking ID and policy numbers — over a large volume of cases would be exhausting and extremely error-prone. Somewhat ironically, this verification step would itself be a great use for another AI tool: one narrowly focused on text comparison between the output letter and the records on file. That kind of comparison is largely mechanical — well-suited to computers generally, and arguably doesn't even require AI. A programmatic diff tool might be more reliable for pure accuracy checking.

06 — Governance

AI Governance — Five-Layer Evaluation

Purpose & Scope

Limited to drafting appeal letters for denied insurance claims when requested by an authorized platform user. No medical advice. No external sending — output posts internally and requires login to view.

Data Access & Privacy

Full access to policy documents. Patient records should be anonymized (like research data) — personal identifiers aren't needed for policy matching and anonymization maintains HIPAA compliance and reduces breach risk.

Human Oversight

Patient-requested appeals require patient review. Hospital-side: a financial threshold or claim category filter could pass routine, lower-urgency appeals through automatically, with higher-stakes ones routed to human review.

Testing, Auditing & Accountability

Track error types and frequency. Any rejected appeal is reviewed post-mortem. Random sampling provides ongoing auditing. Vendor improves the product; hospital bears accountability for choosing to use it.

Monitoring & Sunset

If denial rates on appeals trend upward, the model needs examination. If appeal volume itself rises sharply, insurance policy language may have changed and source data needs updating. These are distinct signals requiring different responses.

07 — Ethics

Should We Be Doing This?

The equity question

Equity is a high concern. I think the gap between large and small institutions will widen initially, but I also think this tool could ultimately become most essential for smaller clinics — those already operating on thinner margins, who lose the most when insurance appeals fail and have the least capacity to fight back manually.

The early adopters will inevitably be large hospitals, both because they can afford it and because they'll help build the technology with their larger data pools. But technology generally becomes cheaper and more accessible over time. A specific mitigation strategy would be a tiered subscription model: lower-cost access for small clinics (who by nature need less volume), and a higher tier for large hospitals. This would help bridge the access gap while the technology matures toward broader affordability.

What this tells us about agentic AI broadly

Healthcare appeals are a good test case precisely because the stakes are high, the documentation requirements are strict, and there's a clear ground truth to audit against. The agent performed well under those constraints — but only because it was given explicit instructions to refuse when evidence was insufficient. The ethical design choice was made at system prompt time, not by the model spontaneously. That's the real lesson: ethics in agentic AI is a design input, not an emergent output.

The agent has three tools: Events (concerts and events this weekend in the Seattle/Bellingham area), Calendar (my weekend schedule), and Budget (remaining entertainment and transportation budget). It cross-references all three to recommend the best option.

System prompt

Concert Weekend Agent — system message
You are a personal event planning assistant. Your goal is to recommend
the best concert or event for me to attend this weekend based on my
schedule, budget, and interests.

Use the Events tool to see what is happening, the Calendar tool to check
when I am free, and the Budget tool to check what I can afford. Recommend
the best option and explain why it fits my schedule and budget. If nothing
works, say so.

Only use facts from the tools. Do not make up events, times, or prices.

My honest take on running this unsupervised

I probably wouldn't let this agent run continuously unsupervised. The level of access needed — constant visibility into my financials, calendar, and potentially location — isn't something I'm comfortable delegating without a lot more thought. If the data could be generalized or anonymized, I'd be more open to it. The same principle applies to the Epic agents mentioned in class: the moment medical data can be decoupled from personal identifiers, I'm much more inclined to trust the system. Personal data needs proportional trust, and trust needs to be earned through demonstrated reliability.

Key Takeaways

  • Agentic AI is only as trustworthy as its initial configuration — Level 3 autonomy demands Level 3 responsibility at setup time.
  • The AI read the insurance policy first, then the patient chart — the reverse of human intuition — and produced a better result because it could internalize the entire policy instantly.
  • When a tool was missing, the agent refused rather than fabricated. This isn't default behavior; it's the result of explicit system prompt design. Ethics is an input, not an output.
  • Verification at scale is its own problem — one that might be better solved programmatically than with another AI layer.
